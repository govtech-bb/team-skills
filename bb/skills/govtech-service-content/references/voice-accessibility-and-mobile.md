# Voice, accessibility and mobile

Use this reference for every task.

It controls the shared GovTech voice, plain language, content accessibility and mobile-first presentation.

Specialist modules may add rules for a page or form type. They must not weaken these shared rules.

## Contents

- Voice, plain language and reading age
- Titles, headings, paragraphs and lists
- Instructions, links and buttons
- Screen-reader and mobile rules
- Critical information and accordions
- Numbers, contact information and sensitive content
- Form microcopy and shared QA

## GovTech voice

Write in clear Standard English.

The voice should feel:

- calm
- practical
- respectful
- direct
- neutral

It should not sound like:

- a ministry memo
- legislation
- a press release
- an advertisement
- political messaging
- a chatbot

Use UK spelling.

Do not use Bajan dialect in official service content.

Do not use em dashes or substitute punctuation that imitates them.

## Plain language

Use:

- short sentences
- active voice
- familiar words
- concrete verbs
- one idea per paragraph
- direct instructions

Prefer:

- get
- use
- need
- show
- send
- fill in
- apply
- start
- contact

Avoid unnecessary:

- administrative terms
- abstract nouns
- formal introductions
- background explanations
- repetition
- reassurance that does not help the user act

Do not remove a necessary legal or official term.

Explain an unfamiliar term in plain language the first time it appears.

Do not repeatedly pair the term with its explanation.

## Reading age

Aim for language a 9-year-old can follow.

A 9-to-11-year-old reading range is acceptable when a complex service or required term makes the lower target impractical.

Reading age is a diagnostic, not permission to:

- change legal meaning
- remove essential conditions
- replace a precise term with a vague one
- make adult content sound childish

When wording remains complex:

1. keep the necessary term
2. simplify the surrounding sentence
3. explain the term once
4. shorten the structure

Do not rely on a reading-age score alone. A low score does not prove that content is clear, accurate or usable.

## Mandatory plain-language pass

After drafting public content, inspect it again only for language difficulty.

For every word or phrase that a member of the public may not use in ordinary conversation:

1. remove it when it adds no meaning
2. replace it with a familiar word when meaning is preserved
3. keep and explain it once when it is legally, technically or officially necessary
4. flag it for content or legal review when a safe explanation cannot be written from the evidence

Check especially for:

- legal and policy terms
- government and administrative language
- acronyms and initialisms
- technical service terms
- abstract nouns made from verbs
- long noun phrases
- Latin expressions
- words copied from legislation or paper forms

Replace avoidable terms such as:

- `applicants desirous of` with a direct instruction using `you`
- `hereby advised` with the information itself
- `requisite` with `needed` or `required`
- `utilise` with `use`
- `commence` with `start`
- `prior to` with `before`
- `subsequent to` with `after`
- `in the event that` with `if`
- `facilitate` with the concrete action it means

Do not use a replacement when it changes a defined legal meaning.

## Legal and official wording

When exact wording is required:

- keep the exact wording separate from the plain-English explanation
- explain what the wording means for the user
- do not claim that the explanation is a legal interpretation unless legal advisers have confirmed it
- do not rewrite a statutory declaration, oath, consent or prescribed statement

If the source uses `shall`, do not automatically copy it or automatically change it. Establish whether the exact wording is prescribed. Use `must` in surrounding public guidance when the obligation is confirmed and plain-language rewriting is permitted.

## Reading-level QA result

For a substantial draft, the internal QA must record one result:

- Pass: ordinary words and short structures are used, with necessary terms explained
- Needs content review: some language could not be simplified safely
- Needs legal or policy confirmation: simplification may change a required meaning

Do not show a numerical reading-age score to a non-content user unless they ask for it. Explain the actual difficult wording instead.

## Titles

Use one H1.

The title should:

- describe the task or subject
- use the words users are likely to recognise
- distinguish the content from related services
- avoid internal programme or system names when a clearer public term exists

Use a task verb for transactional services when appropriate.

Examples:

- Apply for [service]
- Get [document]
- Report [issue]
- Book [appointment]
- Understand [topic]

Do not change an established official service name without identifying the issue.

## Headings

Headings must:

- be short
- be descriptive
- make sense out of context
- help users predict the content below
- use sentence case

Prefer task or question headings when they help:

- Who can apply
- What you need
- How to pay
- What happens next
- Tell us about the applicant

Avoid:

- About
- Details
- Information
- Other
- General
- More

Do not use two headings that perform the same job on one page.

Do not use a heading solely because it appears in a template.

## Paragraphs

Use one idea per paragraph.

Keep paragraphs short enough to scan comfortably on mobile. Around four mobile lines is a useful warning point, not an absolute limit.

Break a paragraph when:

- the user action changes
- a condition changes
- timing or cost must stand out
- a list is easier to scan

Do not create a series of one-sentence paragraphs when the ideas belong together.

## Lists

Use bullets for:

- documents
- preparation items
- eligibility conditions
- route choices
- contact methods
- several fees
- several next steps

Use a lead-in sentence ending with a colon when needed.

Each bullet should normally contain one idea.

Start bullets consistently.

Do not add full stops to short fragments.

Use full stops when bullets are complete sentences.

Do not use decorative bullet symbols or emojis in public copy.

## Instructions

Tell users:

- what to do
- when to do it
- what they need
- what happens after

Put the action before background information where possible.

Do not explain internal reasons unless the explanation changes what the user must do or helps them provide the right information.

## Links

Link text must describe:

- the destination
- the action
- what the user will find

Do not use:

- click here
- read more
- learn more
- this link

Use links for navigation.

Use buttons only for the primary service action or a genuine interaction.

Do not create several competing primary actions.

## Buttons and accessible names

Visible button text should be concise and describe the action.

Use “Start now” for a real transaction when no more specific action is needed.

Use the actual action for:

- Book an appointment
- Sign in
- Download the form
- Contact the department
- Continue

If the visible label is necessarily generic, provide a specific accessible name through an implementation note.

When the same action appears twice on a page:

- both controls must go to the same destination
- both must have the same accessible name

Avoid heading and button combinations that sound repetitive when read aloud.

## Screen-reader content checks

Check:

- one clear H1
- logical heading order
- headings make sense without nearby visual context
- link text makes sense out of context
- button purpose is clear
- instructions do not rely on position such as “above” or “on the right”
- repeated controls are distinguishable or intentionally identical
- error messages identify the question and recovery action
- optional status is available in the label
- important information is not only in an image

Do not use developer notes as a substitute for accessible public content.

## Mobile-first order

Prioritise:

1. what the content lets the user do or understand
2. whether it applies to them, when necessary
3. urgent timing or route blockers
4. service mode
5. the next action
6. practical preparation
7. supporting detail

The exact order depends on user risk.

For service pages, users should normally be able to identify the purpose, service mode, important timing and next action within the first one or two mobile screens.

Do not force all detailed content into this area.

## Critical information

Keep critical information visible without interaction.

Examples:

- deadlines
- loss of entitlement
- route blockers
- attendance requirements
- inability to save
- fees required before proceeding
- safety information

Do not bury critical information in:

- long introductions
- accordions
- help sections
- footnotes
- downloadable documents

## Accordions

Do not use accordions on entry or start pages.

Use accordions in guidance only for supporting conditional detail when:

- the main information remains visible
- users can predict the content from the heading
- the component is keyboard accessible
- expanded and collapsed states are announced properly

Do not put critical information in a closed accordion.

Keep addresses and locations expanded when users need to scan or compare them.

## Numbers, money, dates and time

Use consistent formats across the journey.

State money in a clear Barbados format such as `BDS $100` unless an approved product standard requires another format.

Do not mix currency forms within one service.

Write dates so the month is unambiguous.

Use numerals for:

- fees
- ages
- deadlines
- quantities
- time estimates

Keep units beside numbers.

Do not write a confirmed range as though it were a guarantee.

## Contact information

Include contact information only when it supports a user need.

State the purpose of a contact route.

Keep formatting consistent.

Do not:

- scatter the same details across the page
- invent response times
- include a contact merely because the source contains one
- expose internal contacts publicly

## Tone in sensitive services

Use calm, direct language.

Do not:

- dramatise
- moralise
- blame
- make assumptions about circumstances
- use false reassurance

Prefer language that helps the user act.

For content involving death, illness, disability, money or legal matters, retain necessary precision while removing avoidable formality.

## Content in forms

Question text must be understandable when read independently.

Hint text should:

- remove ambiguity
- prevent a likely error
- explain an unfamiliar term

Do not use hint text to:

- repeat the label
- carry a policy rule
- compensate for a vague question
- provide long guidance

Error messages should:

- identify what went wrong
- tell the user how to fix it
- use the same term as the question
- avoid blame

## Internal notes

Keep internal notes visually and verbally distinct from public copy.

Use approved square-bracket formats.

Do not allow an internal note to interrupt public copy so heavily that the draft cannot be reviewed as a user journey.

For many notes, provide a clean-copy section and a separate issue list.

## Shared QA

Before finalising, check:

- the title matches the task
- there is one H1
- the opening is short and useful
- headings are descriptive
- paragraphs contain one idea
- lists scan easily
- critical information is early and visible
- the primary action is clear
- link and button text is descriptive
- no public/internal audience mixing
- no unnecessary jargon
- every necessary unfamiliar term is explained once
- a separate plain-language pass has been completed for substantial drafts
- no invented reassurance
- terminology is consistent
- money, dates and time are consistent
- content works on mobile
- content makes sense when read aloud
- no empty or duplicated sections
- no unnecessary content remains

Silently correct minor editorial failures.

Report failures that affect accuracy, accessibility, task completion or the service journey.
